Five Things, Four Tiers

Forty-five minutes to get an associate solid on what an IUL does, why it works, and how to sell it honestly at every budget from eighty dollars a month to six figures a year. Ends with a scored check.

Cold open — sell nothing

Before a single fact, the room watches a sale happen without a product being mentioned. Everything else in the next forty-one minutes is an explanation of what just occurred.

Facilitator

Pick one person. Don't warn them. Don't set it up, don't say "I'm going to demonstrate something" — just turn to them and start:

"Can I ask you something most people have never thought about? Do you know that among Americans over seventy-five, the poverty rate for women runs roughly double what it is for men? … Any idea why?"

Run the Widow's Chain start to finish — all eight beats, from the couple at thirty to the question at the end. Stop dead at "who pays?" and let the silence sit. Do not rescue it. When they answer, let the answer land on the room before you move.

Then, to everyone: "What did I just sell?"

Answer: nothing. No product, no number, no illustration. Say that out loud — it's the thesis of the whole course.

On your own

Open the manual to §03, The Widow's Chain, and read all eight beats out loud — actually out loud, not in your head. Time yourself. Then answer honestly: at which beat did you feel it turn?

Notice what isn't in it. No product, no cap, no illustration, no number. That's not an accident, and it's the thing most agents never figure out.

You've got it when The room can state what made the sale — a question they answered themselves — rather than what was presented to them.

The five things

If someone remembers one thing from this course, it's these five. Not features — these. Every objection, every tier, and every close routes back through them.

  1. Permanent coverage after the term expires. Term is a rented fence around your worst twenty years. It ends.
  2. Long-term care that protects the assets you built. A care event is the most efficient way ever devised to liquidate a lifetime of savings.
  3. Growth without state, federal, or capital gains tax. Structured properly and kept in force.
  4. Growth without exposure to market loss. The index credit has a floor.
  5. A tax-advantaged family bank. No application, no credit check, no job required, no stated purpose.

The close is never "do you want an IUL." It's "how many of those five matter to you?" You sell the five things. The product is simply the only vehicle that does all of them at once.

Drill · 4 minutes

Round 1: everyone closes the page and writes all five from memory. Most people get three.

Round 2: read them again, close, write again. Now most get five.

Round 3: partner up. One person names a thing, the other has to produce the question that opens it — the questions are in §02 of the manual. Switch after every one.

Facilitator

Don't skip round two because round one went badly — round one always goes badly, and that's the point of the exercise. Ask for a show of hands on how many people got all five the first time. The gap between what people think they know and what they can produce cold is the entire reason this segment exists.

You've got it when You can produce all five, in any order, with no notes, plus the question that opens each one.
0:127 minutes

Why it works — and saying it precisely

Mechanism doesn't sell. But vagueness kills, and one careless phrase creates real compliance exposure. Thirty seconds of explanation, said exactly right.

Rock and paper

Fist on the table for the rock, flat hand for the paper.

The rock is bonds. The carrier holds the bulk of the money in its general account. Bonds don't track the index — whatever the market does this year, the rock is still sitting there.

The paper is options. The carrier spends the interest the rock throws off on options that pay if the index rises. It buys upside with the yield, never with the principal.

Market up: the paper pays and the gain is credited on top of the rock. Market down: the paper expires worthless. You lost the interest you spent on it. You did not lose the rock.

Drill · 3 minutes

Partner up. Thirty seconds each, hands on the table, explain the floor to your partner as if they were a client. Then switch. Then do it again — the second attempt is always half the length and twice as clear.

The line that gets you in trouble

The floor applies to the index credit, not to the account value. Cost of insurance, admin and rider charges still come out in a zero year, so cash value can decline.

Never say: "You can't lose money."
Say: "Your money isn't exposed to market losses — the index credit has a zero floor. Policy charges still come out, so let me show you both columns."

Facilitator

Make the room say the correct version out loud, together, once. It feels silly and it's the reason they'll say it right in front of a client six weeks from now. Muscle memory beats a bullet point.

You've got it when You can explain the floor in thirty seconds without saying "can't lose money," and you volunteer the charges before the client asks.

The market ladder

The same five things sell at eighty dollars a month and at three hundred thousand a year — but not the same way, and sometimes not as an IUL at all. This is the segment that prevents the two most common failures: running an affluent script at a young family, and forcing a policy on someone who needs term.

Three questions place the client — they matter more than income

  1. Is the death benefit need actually covered? If a death this year would wreck the household and the coverage isn't there, that gets solved first — at every income level.
  2. What can they fund every month for twenty years without resenting it? Not what they can afford this month. What survives a slow quarter and a new roof.
  3. Do they own assets a care event would liquidate? This decides whether long-term care is an affordability conversation or an asset-protection one.

Tier 1

Protection first

roughly $50–150 / month

Lead with
The need calculation, the right amount of term, and convertibility.
Give them
Insurability. "The most valuable thing I can hand you at twenty-eight is the right to buy a policy later at today's health."
Never
Sell a small IUL instead of adequate term. The commission looks similar. That's exactly why the temptation exists.

Tier 2

The core household

roughly $200–500 / month

Lead with
The Widow's Chain, then things 1 and 2 — coverage that outlives the term, and care that protects the house.
Size it
For the twenty-year number, not the maximum. Say out loud that you're applying the brakes.
Not yet
Banking. At $300/month the cash value isn't real capital for eight to ten years. Overselling it creates a year-four cancellation.

Tier 3

The builder

roughly $1,000–3,000 / month

Lead with
Sequence of returns and tax diversification. Care becomes asset protection.
Now real
The family bank. Usable capital by years five to eight, and this client has actual uses for it.
Vocabulary
Non-correlated asset, participating vs. wash loans. Fumbling a term costs you the case here.

Tier 4

The affluent case

$3,000+ / month, into six figures

Lead with
"You've maxed everything they'll let you max. Where does the rest of the after-tax money go?" Three buckets, IRMAA, RMDs, the ten-year rule.
Position
Against their bonds, never their stocks.
Non-negotiable
Max-funded to the seven-pay limit, minimum non-MEC death benefit, guaranteed column first, straight answer on how design affects your pay.

When not to sell an IUL — at any income level

  • They can't sustain the funding through a bad year.
  • They need the money inside five years.
  • The death benefit need is unmet and the budget is finite — term first, always.
  • They haven't captured the employer match. Free money outranks your product.
  • Underwriting rates them so heavily the charges break the design.
  • High-interest consumer debt is sitting there. Nothing here beats retiring a 24% balance.
Drill · 4 minutes

Read each of these out and have the room call the tier — or call "not an IUL":

  1. 29, two kids under four, $120 a month, no coverage but a small group policy at work.
  2. Surgeon, 41, $900K income, maxed 403(b), $300K group life, three kids.
  3. Couple, 38, two incomes, mortgage, $400 a month available, aging parents with no plan.
  4. Contractor, 45, owns his business, $2,500 a month available, $18,000 on a credit card at 22%.
  5. 33, single, $1,800 a month available, planning to buy a house in two years.

Answers: 1 — Tier 1, term with convertibility. 2 — Tier 4, but check the coverage gap first; three kids on $300K of group is a Tier 1 problem in a Tier 4 suit. 3 — Tier 2, and the parent play is live. 4 — retire the card first, then Tier 3. 5 — not now; they need the money inside five years.

Facilitator

Case 2 and case 4 are the ones that teach. Most rooms call case 2 "Tier 4" instantly and skip the coverage gap — let them, then point it out. And someone will always try to sell case 4 a policy anyway. Ask them what happens to that client's opinion of us in eighteen months.

On your own

Work the five cases above before reading the answers. Then pull your own last five appointments and tier each one honestly. If any of them were tiered wrong, that's your list of follow-up calls this week.

You've got it when You can tier a prospect from three questions, and you can say "you shouldn't buy this" without flinching.

Objection reps

Nobody learns to handle an objection by reading a handled objection. This segment is reps, out loud, under mild time pressure — because that's the only condition that resembles the field.

Drill · the whole segment

Pairs. One is the client, one is the agent. The client picks an objection and delivers it flat and a little rude, the way a real one arrives. The agent has thirty seconds to answer with a question, not a defense. Switch. Six rounds, then rotate partners and run the six again.

  1. "Permanent insurance has way too many fees."
  2. "Buy term and invest the difference."
  3. "I'd rather max my 401(k) — I need the deduction now."
  4. "My CPA says IULs are a bad idea."
  5. "Real estate is my investment. I don't need this."
  6. "Those illustrations look unrealistic to me."

Full answers are in §12 of the manual, but don't read them during the drill. Answer badly first. The correction sticks better than the script.

The pattern under all six

  • Concede the true part immediately. "You should have term." "Max the 401(k)." "You should have a CPA with opinions." Agreement disarms; argument entrenches.
  • Answer with a question. "Which building would you sell?" beats any paragraph you could deliver.
  • Never defend the product. Return to the five things and ask which ones they want.
Facilitator

Walk the room. The tell that someone is doing it wrong is length — if the answer runs past thirty seconds they're defending, not asking. Call it out gently in the moment; don't save it for the debrief.

On your own

Record voice memos. Deliver each objection to yourself, then answer in thirty seconds, then play it back. It's uncomfortable and it works faster than anything else in this course.

You've got it when Every one of the six gets answered in under thirty seconds, ending in a question, without defending anything.

The three phrases

Most of the guardrails in the manual protect your credibility. These three protect your license. Teach only these three live; the rest is reference.

1 · "It can't lose money"

The index credit has a floor. The account value still absorbs policy charges. Say "your money isn't exposed to market losses" and show both columns.

2 · "It's an investment"

An IUL is life insurance, not a security. Say strategy, asset, policy, credited interest, accumulation. Never returns, never the best investment, never I'll invest this for you.

3 · "It's tax-free"

Conditionally. The policy has to stay in force and stay outside MEC status. A lapse with a large outstanding loan creates a tax bill on money the client no longer has. Say "tax-advantaged — as long as it's structured properly and stays in force."

Two more that will date you or embarrass you

  • Step-up in basis was never repealed. That was a 2021 proposal that died. It's still in effect, and the estate exemption is $15 million per person in 2026 and permanent. Saying otherwise loses the case the moment their CPA hears it.
  • Social Security: 2034, about 83% payable — not "bankrupt," not 2028. Your prospect can check this on their phone while you're still talking.
Facilitator

Frame this as protection, not policing. The three phrases exist because the people who say them are usually the people who believe hardest — enthusiasm is what produces the sloppy sentence. Say that; the room will take the correction better.

You've got it when You catch yourself mid-sentence and self-correct in front of a client. That's the moment they start trusting you.
