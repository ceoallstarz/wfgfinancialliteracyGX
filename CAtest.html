How to run a session

For whoever is sitting next to him. Read this once before the first session.

Ask this before you teach anything

"When you say the licensing language feels like Chinese — what's hardest? The vocabulary, understanding what the question is asking, remembering it, or all three?"

His answer sets the plan, not his age:

  • Vocabulary → live in this tool. Ladder rungs 1 and 2. Skip the quizzes for the first two weeks.
  • Understanding the question → he knows more than he thinks. Go straight to quiz mode and read every question out loud together, translating the question stem before he answers.
  • Remembering → short and frequent beats long. Three 15-minute sittings a week beats one three-hour Saturday.
  • All three → one module a week, in order. Ten weeks. Say it that way so it has an end.

The 20-minute sitting

  • 2 min — he re-says three terms from last time, out loud, from memory. No cards.
  • 12 min — five to eight new cards. He reads the term, guesses, then climbs the ladder.
  • 4 min — he teaches the five back to you like you've never heard them.
  • 2 min — name what got easier. Out loud. Specifically.

What you say and what you don't

  • Never: "Does that make sense?" — he'll say yes to end the discomfort.
  • Instead: "Say it back to me the way you'd say it to Mom."
  • Never: "We covered that already."
  • Instead: "Good — that one's worth hearing twice. Run it again."
  • Never: praise only the score.
  • Instead: "Two weeks ago 'aleatory' was noise. You just explained it cold."

Why the cards work this way

Three rungs, always in the same order. Plain English first, because a definition written in exam language just adds more foreign words. Real life second, because the student has a lifetime of examples the exam doesn't know about. Test language last, and only last — once he already owns the idea, the exam's phrasing is a translation problem, not a comprehension problem.

The tool never asks whether he understood. It asks him to say it out loud, then he rates himself. "Say it again" is a button with no penalty attached — pressing it is the tool working correctly.

Where the points are

150 questions, 60% to pass. They are not spread evenly — Life and Health carry the test:

  • Life insurance — the single biggest block. Modules 5, 6, 7, and 9 feed it.
  • Medical / health insurance — nearly as big. Modules 10 and 12 feed it.
  • General insurance — basics and contract law. Modules 1, 2, 3, 11.
  • Disability, long-term care, riders — smaller blocks, a handful of questions each.

Coach the big blocks to mastery first. A point in Life counts the same as a point in LTC, and there are far more of them.

The four kinds of question

Teach them to sort every question into one of four buckets the moment they read it:

  • The "I know this" → answer it, move on, don't overthink it.
  • The 50/50 → cross off the one or two clearly wrong options, then trust the gut pick. Tell them plainly: do not change a 50/50 answer later — changes usually go from right to wrong.
  • The "no idea" → pick the option that stands out most, flag it, move on, come back at the end. Never sit and stew.
  • The teaching question → two to five questions will teach you something you didn't know. Note what you learned; it often answers a "no idea" question later in the same test.

The one exam-day rule

Never leave a question blank.

Wrong answers cost nothing beyond the point — there is no penalty for guessing. A blank is the only choice with a zero percent chance of being right. If time is running out, fill every remaining bubble with something. And when reviewing at the end, only revisit the ones left blank or purely guessed — leave the confident answers alone.

From the trainers who've done this a thousand times

  • Book the test now. The material sticks for about a day. Finish the study, then take the exam within a few days — before work, kids, and life erode it. Don't finish the class and "get to it next month."
  • Passing is the goal, not acing. California needs 60%. If someone scores 100%, they over-studied by 40%. Don't let anyone disappear for months chasing a perfect score — pass and go help families.
  • Guard the environment. Testing from home means truly distraction-free: a proctor who sees someone walk behind the screen, or a kid or a dog interrupting, can fail the attempt. If home isn't controllable, use a test center.
  • Trust the first instinct. There's a reason it looked right the first time. Flag-and-return is fine for genuine unknowns; changing a confident answer is how a pass becomes a fail.
